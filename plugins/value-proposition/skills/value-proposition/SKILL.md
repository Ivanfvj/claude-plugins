A value proposition is the reason a specific customer picks you over their **alternatives**, written in their words, provable, and clear in five seconds. It comes out of research; drafting is the last third of the work. The method below joins five sources (Copyhackers, April Dunford, Strategyzer, Bain/HBR, NN/g + Unbounce); quotes and links live in [REFERENCE.md](REFERENCE.md).

Reviewing an existing one: run it through the checklist in step 6 first, report which checks fail, then rework only the steps those failures point to.

## 1. Rule of one

Pick **one customer** and **one offer**. Name the segment by a situation they are in ("movers serving 80+ towns"), not a demographic. Best source: the customers who already love the product and why they bought.

Done when: one segment and one offer are written down. A second segment gets its own value proposition.

## 2. Collect customer words

Gather the customer's exact phrases for their **jobs**, **pains** and **gains** (Strategyzer's canvas). Sources, best first: interviews and sales calls the user can share, support tickets, the product's own reviews, competitor reviews (G2, Capterra, App Store, Reddit), the user's own knowledge. Ladder each stated preference ("convenient") down to what drives it ("saves time, avoids hassle").

Done when: a list of verbatim phrases with their source. Anything that came from the user's opinion or yours is tagged **Assumption**. If the list is thin, say so and offer interview questions before drafting on assumptions.

## 3. Map the alternatives

Ask: what would this customer do if the product did not exist? Group the answers into 2–5 **alternatives**, and always include doing it by hand and doing nothing. Then list the attributes the product has that those alternatives lack. Keep only attributes with **proof** (a number, a capability, a customer result); opinions such as "great support" are dropped.

Done when: a table of alternatives × proven attributes.

## 4. Turn attributes into value

For each attribute, write the chain attribute → benefit → value, where value is the customer goal it serves. Ask "who cares, and why?" of each. Name the value precisely; the Bain elements list in [REFERENCE.md](REFERENCE.md) gives the vocabulary (consumer research; for B2B lean on the alternatives table and the economic outcome).

Narrow to one or two values, three at most. A generic claim like "we save time" is a **placeholder**: replace it with how much, compared with what, for whom.

Done when: 1–3 values, each tied to a proven attribute and to a phrase from step 2.

## 5. Draft many, then pick

Write 20+ headline variants across these formulas, reusing the customer's phrases:

- "My product is the one that ______."
- "[Desired outcome] by [how to get it]"
- "[Do something hard] in [period of time]"
- "Helping [segment] do [job]."
- "[Category] for [segment]" (Dunford: "team collaboration for lawyers")
- "Like a ______. Unlike any ______."

Shape the winner as a unit: a **headline** (what it is and for whom), a **subhead** (specifics: number, mechanism, scope), 3 **proof bullets**, and a suggested visual. Clear beats clever.

Done when: 20+ variants exist and the top 3 are shaped as full units.

## 6. Checklist

Score each top candidate. It ships only when every row passes; a failing row sends you back to the step in brackets.

1. Names a specific segment, not everyone [1]
2. Unique: competitors can't or don't say it [3]
3. Desirable: the customer wants the unique part (it appears in their words) [2]
4. Specific: a number, a niche, or a concrete mechanism [4]
5. Succinct: longer than a 4-word tagline, shorter than a mission statement [5]
6. Sticky: a reader could recall the gist after one read [5]
7. Beats the next best alternative, including doing nothing [3]
8. The pain relieved is worth more than the price [4]
9. The difference is visible to the customer, not just to competitors [3]
10. Every claim is provable [3]
11. Answers "why choose this over the others?" [4]
12. Plain words, no jargon or feature-speak [5]
13. Understood at a glance by someone scanning [5]

Rewrite on sight: a feature list, a mission statement, a slogan, a welcome line, a sentence that could sit on any company's site.

## 7. Test plan

Hand the user a test, since only real buyers can pass step 6's rows 3, 6 and 13:

- **5-second test**: show the hero for 5 seconds, ask "what does this do, and who is it for?" (Unbounce saw 6% correct on a generic headline.)
- **Buyer feedback**: 5–10 people from the segment rate the top 3 for clarity and relevance (interviews, or a paid panel such as Wynter).
- **Live**: A/B the headline once traffic allows.

## Deliver

1. The winning unit (headline, subhead, 3 bullets, visual note) and the 2 runners-up.
2. The checklist scores for all three.
3. The alternatives × attributes table and the 1–3 chosen values, each with its proof.
4. Every Assumption listed, and the test plan.

Once validated, keep it stable until the product, market or competitors change; restlessness inside the team is not a reason to rewrite.
